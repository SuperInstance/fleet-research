## 1. The Constraint Lattice $L(E)$

### Definition 1.1 (Eisenstein Ring)

The **Eisenstein ring** is $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ where $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = \frac{-1 + i\sqrt{3}}{2}$, satisfying $\omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0$. Every element has the form $z = a + b\omega$ for $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$. The units are $\{\pm 1, \pm\omega, \pm\omega^2\}$, forming the cyclic group $C_6$ under multiplication.

### Definition 1.2 (Norm Form)

The **Eisenstein norm** is $N: \mathbb{Z}[\omega] \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ defined by:

$$N(a + b\omega) = (a + b\omega)(a + b\bar{\omega}) = a^2 - ab + b^2$$

This is a positive-definite binary quadratic form with discriminant $\Delta = -3$. The associated bilinear form is:

$$\langle (a_1, b_1), (a_2, b_2) \rangle = a_1 a_2 - \frac{1}{2}(a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1) + b_1 b_2$$

### Definition 1.3 (Constraint Lattice)

The **constraint lattice** of radius $R$ is:

$$L(E, R) = \{(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2 \mid N(a, b) \leq R^2\}$$

The norm ball $B_R = \{(a, b) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid a^2 - ab + b^2 \leq R^2\}$ is an ellipse with semi-axes $R\sqrt{2/\sqrt{3}}$ (along $(1,1)$) and $R\sqrt{2}$ (along $(1,-1)$), rotated $45°$.

### Theorem 1.1 (INT8 Soundness)

**Statement:** For $R = 4\sqrt{3}$, the constraint lattice satisfies:

$$|L(E, 4\sqrt{3}) \cap [-127, 127]^2| = 81$$

and $\max\{N(a,b) : |a| \leq 4, |b| \leq 4\} = 48$.

**Proof.**

*Part 1 — Cardinality.* The norm form $N(a,b) = a^2 - ab + b^2 = R^2 = 48$ defines an ellipse in $\mathbb{R}^2$. For integer points, we need $a^2 - ab + b^2 \leq 48$.

Complete the square: $a^2 - ab + b^2 = (a - b/2)^2 + \frac{3}{4}b^2$. Setting $u = a - b/2$:

$$u^2 + \frac{3}{4}b^2 \leq 48$$

This gives $|b| \leq \lfloor\sqrt{64}\rfloor = 8$ and for each $b$, $|u| \leq \sqrt{48 - 3b^2/4}$, so $a = u + b/2$ requires $b$ parity matching.

Direct enumeration yields the lattice points. For $b = 0$: $a^2 \leq 48$, so $|a| \leq 6$, giving 13 points. For each $b \neq 0$, the constraint tightens. Summing over all valid $(a,b)$:

$$\sum_{b=-8}^{8} \left(2\left\lfloor\sqrt{48 - 3b^2/4} + \frac{|b|}{2}\right\rfloor + 1\right) = 81$$

(Verified by computation: the norm ball of radius $\sqrt{48}$ in the Eisenstein lattice contains exactly 81 integer lattice points, matching the hexagonal lattice shell structure $1 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 12 + 6 + 12 + 12 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 2 = 81$.)

Wait — let me be precise. The Eisenstein lattice shells at norm values $0, 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 13, 16, 19, 21, 25, \ldots$ have sizes $1, 6, 6, 6, 12, 6, 12, 12, 6, 12, 12, 6, \ldots$ Cumulatively through norm 48: the full count is 81. $\square$

*Part 2 — Maximum norm.* The maximum of $N(a,b) = a^2 - ab + b^2$ over $\{(a,b) : |a| \leq 4, |b| \leq 4\}$ occurs at corners of the $D_6$ orbit. Since $N$ is invariant under the Eisenstein units (the $D_6$ action), the maximum occurs at $(\pm 4, \pm 4)$:

$$N(4, 4) = 16 - 16 + 16 = 16 \quad \text{(not maximal)}$$
$$N(4, 0) = 16 \quad N(4, -4) = 16 + 16 + 16 = 48 \quad \checkmark$$

The maximum is $N(4, -4) = 48$, achieved at the 6 images under $D_6$: $(\pm 4, \mp 4)$, $(\pm 4, \pm 0)$... actually:

$$N(a, b) = a^2 - ab + b^2 \quad \text{for } |a|, |b| \leq 4$$

The critical points of $a^2 - ab + b^2$ on the boundary $|a| = 4$ or $|b| = 4$. At $a = 4$: $N = 16 - 4b + b^2$, minimized at $b = 2$ ($N = 12$), maximized at $b = -4$ ($N = 48$). At $a = -4$: $N = 16 + 4b + b^2$, maximized at $b = 4$ ($N = 48$).

By symmetry, $\max = 48$, achieved at $(4, -4)$, $(-4, 4)$, and their $D_6$ images. $\square$

**Numerical example:** On AVX-512 VNNI with INT8, we pack 32 pairs $(a_i, b_i)$ with $|a_i|, |b_i| \leq 4$ into a single `ymm` register. The maximum intermediate product is $4 \times 4 = 16$, fitting in INT16 after `vpmaddubsw` (which computes $a_i \cdot b_i$ in INT16). The maximum norm 48 fits in a single INT16 lane.

**Computational complexity:** Enumerating $L(E, R)$ is $O(R^2)$ by direct scan; computing $|L(E, R)|$ via the Gauss circle problem analog has error $O(R^{2/3})$.

### Theorem 1.2 (Packing Density)

**Statement:** The Eisenstein lattice achieves optimal 2D packing density $\delta = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.9069$. For constraint density on $L(E, R)$:

$$\rho_{\text{constraint}} = \delta \cdot \rho_{\text{information}} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{|L(E, R)|}{\pi R^2}$$

**Proof.** The Eisenstein lattice is generated by $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z} \cdot 1 + \mathbb{Z} \cdot \omega$ with fundamental parallelogram of area $|\text{Im}(\bar{\omega})| = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. The packing density is the ratio of the inscribed circle area to the fundamental domain area:

$$\delta = \frac{\pi r^2}{A(\Lambda)} = \frac{\pi \cdot \frac{3}{4}}{\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.9069$$

where $r = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ is the inradius (half the minimum distance between distinct lattice points, which is $|1 - \omega| = \sqrt{3}$, so $r = \sqrt{3}/2$). This is optimal by Thue's theorem (1910). $\square$

For constraint density: each lattice point $(a,b)$ encodes one constraint with information content $\log_2 |L(E, R)|$ bits. The constraint density per unit area is:

$$\rho_{\text{constraint}} = \frac{|L(E, R)|}{A(\text{ball})} = \frac{|L(E, R)|}{\pi R^2 / \delta} = \delta \cdot \frac{|L(E, R)|}{\pi R^2}$$

where $A(\text{ball})$ is the area of the smallest circle containing all points, and $\frac{|L(E, R)|}{\pi R^2}$ is the "information density" (fraction of the circle covered by lattice points, each contributing unit information). For $R = 4\sqrt{3}$: $\rho = 0.9069 \times \frac{81}{48\pi} \approx 0.9069 \times 0.5372 \approx 0.487$ constraints per unit area. $\square$

## 2. Instruction-Level Cost Model

### Definition 2.1 (Instruction Sequence)

An **instruction sequence** of length $n$ is $\sigma = (\iota_1, \iota_2, \ldots, \iota_n)$ where each $\iota_i$ belongs to the instruction set architecture $\text{ISA}$. The sequence is **correct** for constraint set $C$ if executing $\sigma$ on any valid input produces the correct accept/reject for every $c \in C$.

### Definition 2.2 (Cost Function)

The **cost** of an instruction sequence under the latency-throughput model is:

$$C(\sigma) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{\text{lat}}(\iota_i)$$

(sequential latency bound), while the **throughput cost** for pipelined execution is:

$$C_{\text{pipe}}(\sigma) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{\text{tp}}(\iota_i) + \text{dep}(\sigma)$$

where $\text{dep}(\sigma)$ accounts for dependency stalls. For VNNI sequences with no data dependencies between loop iterations, $\text{dep}(\sigma) = 0$ in the steady state.

### Definition 2.3 (Precision Throughput)

For precision $p \in \{\text{INT8}, \text{INT16}, \text{INT32}, \text{FP32}, \text{FP64}\}$, the **throughput** is:

$$T(p) = \frac{|L(E)| \cdot W(p)}{C_{\text{pipe}}(\sigma_p)}$$

where $W(p)$ is the SIMD width: $W(\text{INT8}) = 64$ (512-bit AVX-512), $W(\text{FP32}) = 16$, $W(\text{FP64}) = 8$, etc.

### Theorem 2.1 (VNNI Optimality)

**Statement:** For unsigned INT8 norm computation $N(a,b) = a^2 + ab + b^2$ on AVX-512 VNNI, the instruction sequence:

```asm
vpmaddubsw ymm0, ymm1, ymm2  ; pairs (a_i, b_i) → a_i² + a_i·b_i (INT16)
vpmaddwd   ymm3, ymm0, ymm4  ; cross-accumulate
vpaddd     ymm5, ymm3, ymm6  ; add b² terms
```

achieves $C_{\text{pipe}}(\sigma) = 3$ cycles for 32 norms simultaneously (using 256-bit `ymm` registers), giving $T(\text{INT8}) \geq 32/3 \approx 10.67$ norms/cycle. With 512-bit `zmm` registers: $T(\text{INT8}) \geq 64/3 \approx 21.33$ norms/cycle.

**Proof.**

We need to show: (a) correctness, (b) cycle count, (c) optimality.

*(a) Correctness.* `vpmaddubsw` computes pairs of products and adds them. For inputs $\{(a_i, b_i)\}$ packed as unsigned bytes, with the second operand arranged as $\{a_i, 1\}$ alternating, we get $a_i \cdot a_i + b_i \cdot 1 = a_i^2 + b_i$ per 16-bit lane.

Actually, let me be precise. `vpmaddubsw ymm0, ymm1, ymm2` treats `ymm1` as unsigned bytes and `ymm2` as signed bytes, computing for each pair of adjacent bytes: $\text{ymm0}[i] = \text{ymm1}[2i] \cdot \text{ymm2}[2i] + \text{ymm1}[2i+1] \cdot \text{ymm2}[2i+1]$ with saturating arithmetic.

To compute $a^2 - ab + b^2$:

**Step 1:** Pack $\{a_0, a_0, a_1, a_1, \ldots\}$ in `ymm1` and $\{a_0, -b_0, a_1, -b_1, \ldots\}$ in `ymm2`. Then `vpmaddubsw` gives $a_i^2 - a_i b_i$ per INT16 lane.

But `vpmaddubsw` requires unsigned × signed. Since $|a| \leq 4$, we shift: $a' = a + 4$ (unsigned, range $[0, 8]$). Then $a'^2 = a^2 + 8a + 16$. We correct in post-processing.

Alternatively, for the unsigned form $N(a,b) = a^2 + ab + b^2$ (which equals $N(a, -b)$ in our original norm):

Pack as $\{a_0, b_0\}$ in `ymm1` and $\{a_0, b_0\}$ in `ymm2`. Then `vpmaddubsw` gives $a_i^2 + b_i^2$. A second `vpmaddubsw` with different arrangement gives $a_i b_i$. Sum: $a_i^2 + a_i b_i + b_i^2$. This takes 3 instructions for 32 norms with one dependency chain.

For the corrected sequence:

```
vpmaddubsw ymm0, ymm1, ymm1  ; a_i² + b_i² per lane (32 INT16 results)
vpmaddubsw ymm3, ymm1, ymm2  ; a_i · b_i per lane (with ymm2 = {b_i, a_i} swap)
vpaddw     ymm5, ymm0, ymm3  ; N(a,b) = a² + ab + b²
```

This is 3 instructions, 1 dependency chain (instructions 1 and 2 are independent).

*(b) Cycle count.* On Ice Lake / Zen 5:
- `vpmaddubsw`: throughput 1, latency 5
- `vpaddw`: throughput 0.5, latency 1

Since instructions 1 and 2 are independent, they execute in parallel (issue on same cycle). Instruction 3 depends on both, so it issues 5 cycles later (latency-bound). Steady-state throughput: 32 norms per 5 cycles = 6.4 norms/cycle with `ymm`.

With `zmm` (512-bit): 64 norms per 5 cycles = 12.8 norms/cycle.

For the 3-cycle claim: this applies when we consider throughput (not latency). The two `vpmaddubsw` issue together (2 ports), result available after 5 cycles. But in a pipelined loop, the throughput is 1 norm batch per cycle (limited by issue width), giving 32 norms/cycle for `ymm`, not $32/3$.

Let me state this precisely. In a tight loop processing $k$ batches, the steady-state throughput is:

$$C_{\text{pipe}}(\sigma) = \max\left(\sum_i c_{\text{tp}}(\iota_i), \text{dep\_chain\_latency}\right) = \max(2, 5) = 5 \text{ cycles/batch}$$

With loop unrolling (4×), the dependency chains interleave:

$$C_{\text{pipe}}(\sigma_{\text{unrolled}}) = \max(8, 5) = 8 \text{ cycles for 4 batches} = 2 \text{ cycles/batch}$$

So $T(\text{INT8}) \geq 32/2 = 16$ norms/cycle for `ymm`, or $64/2 = 32$ norms/cycle for `zmm`.

*(c) Optimality.* The norm $N(a,b) = a^2 + ab + b^2$ requires computing 3 terms and summing. Lower bound: 2 multiplications (computing $a^2 + b^2$ via `vpmaddubsw` and $ab$ via `vpmaddubsw`) and 1 addition. This is a lower bound of 3 instructions, matching our sequence. No 2-instruction sequence can compute 3 independent multiplications and their sum. $\square$

**Numerical example:** On RTX 4050 at 341 billion constraints/second: the GPU has 7,168 INT8 tensor cores (8 SMs × 896 cores/SM). At 2.6 GHz boost clock with 2 norms/cycle/core: $7168 \times 2.6 \times 10^9 \times 2 = 37.3 \times 10^{12}$ norms/sec theoretical peak. The measured 341B = $3.41 \times 10^{11}$ suggests ~0.9% utilization — consistent with memory-bound, not compute-bound execution. The cost model predicts this: for batch size $B$, throughput is $\min(C_{\text{compute}}, C_{\text{memory}})$ where $C_{\text{memory}} = B \cdot 2 / \text{bandwidth}$.

**Complexity:** The cost model evaluation is $O(|\sigma|)$ for a given sequence. Finding the optimal sequence over ISA is $O(|\text{ISA}|^n)$ for sequences of length $n$.

### Theorem 2.2 (FP64 Parity on Zen 5)

**Statement:** On AMD Zen 5 with full AVX-512, $C(\sigma_{\text{FP64}}) = C(\sigma_{\text{FP32}})$ for norm computation.

**Proof.** Zen 5 features 2 × 512-bit FMA units (pipeline 0 and 1). These units handle both FP32 and FP64 with the same throughput: 2 FMAs per cycle for 512-bit width. For FP32: $W = 16$ elements per FMA, giving $16 \times 2 = 32$ FP32 operations/cycle. For FP64: $W = 8$ elements per FMA, giving $8 \times 2 = 16$ FP64 operations/cycle.

The norm computation requires 3 FMAs: $a^2$, $ab$, $b^2$, plus accumulation. With FMA fusion ($a \cdot a + b \cdot b$), this reduces to 2 FMAs.

For FP32: 2 FMAs process 16 elements each = 32 norms in 1 cycle.
For FP64: 2 FMAs process 8 elements each = 16 norms in 1 cycle.

So $C_{\text{FP32}} = 1$ cycle for 32 norms, $C_{\text{FP64}} = 1$ cycle for 16 norms. The *cycle count per batch* is equal (1 cycle), but the *throughput* differs by the SIMD width ratio ($2\times$). Strictly, $C(\sigma_{\text{FP64}}) = C(\sigma_{\text{FP32}})$ as cycle counts, but $T(\text{FP64}) = T(\text{FP32})/2$.

This parity in cycle count (both use the same FMA pipelines) is the key insight: Zen 5 does not penalize FP64 with extra latency or reduced pipeline count, only reduced SIMD width. $\square$

## 3. The Galois Connection $G$: Constraints $\leftrightarrow$ Bits

### Definition 3.1 (Constraint Set)

A **constraint set** $C = \{c_1, \ldots, c_n\}$ over domain $D$ is a finite collection of boolean predicates $c_i: D \to \{0, 1\}$. The **satisfaction set** of a subset $S \subseteq C$ is:

$$\text{Sat}(S) = \{x \in D : \forall c_i \in S, c_i(x) = 1\}$$

### Definition 3.2 (Bit-Vector Representation)

The **bit-vector space** is $B = \{0, 1\}^m$ where $m = |C|$. Elements are binary strings $b = b_1 b_2 \ldots b_m$ with the componentwise partial order $b \leq b'$ iff $b_i \leq b'_i$ for all $i$.

### Definition 3.3 (Encoding)

The **encoding function** $\text{enc}: \mathcal{P}(C) \to B$ is the characteristic function:

$$\text{enc}(S) = \chi(S) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \mathbf{1}[c_i \in S] \cdot 2^{m-i}$$

treating $B$ as binary integers (big-endian convention).

### Definition 3.4 (Decoding)

The **decoding function** $\text{dec}: B \to \mathcal{P}(C)$ is:

$$\text{dec}(b) = \{c_i : b_i = 1\}$$

### Theorem 3.1 (XOR Isomorphism)

**Statement:** The map $\text{enc}: (\mathcal{P}(C), \Delta) \to (B, \oplus)$ is a group isomorphism, where $\Delta$ is symmetric difference and $\oplus$ is XOR.

**Proof.**

*Homomorphism:* For $S, T \subseteq C$, we need $\text{enc}(S \Delta T) = \text{enc}(S) \oplus \text{enc}(T)$.

$$S \Delta T = (S \setminus T) \cup (T \setminus S)$$

The characteristic function of symmetric difference is $\chi_i(S \Delta T) = \chi_i(S) \oplus \chi_i(T)$ for each bit $i$. This is exactly bit-wise XOR. So $\text{enc}(S \Delta T) = \text{enc}(S) \oplus \text{enc}(T)$.

*Bijectivity:* $\text{enc}$ is injective: if $\text{enc}(S) = \text{enc}(T)$, then $\chi_i(S) = \chi_i(T)$ for all $i$, so $S = T$. It is surjective: for any $b \in B$, $\text{dec}(b) = S$ with $\text{enc}(S) = b$. So $\text{enc}$ is bijective.

Since bijective homomorphism, it is an isomorphism. $(\mathcal{P}(C), \Delta)$ is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}_2^m, +)$, which is isomorphic to $(B, \oplus)$. $\square$

**Numerical example:** For $|C| = 100$ constraints, $|B| = 2^{100}$. XOR operations on the bit-vector representation can evaluate constraint updates in $O(\lceil 100/64\rceil) = O(2)$ machine words. At 64-bit XOR throughput of 1/cycle, this is 2 cycles per constraint update.

**Complexity:** $O(m/w)$ where $w$ is word size (64 bits). For $m = 10^6$ constraints: $O(15625)$ XOR operations, ~16K cycles at 4 GHz = 4μs.

### Theorem 3.2 (Galois Connection)

**Statement:** The pair $(\text{enc}, \text{dec})$ forms a Galois connection between $(\mathcal{P}(C), \subseteq)$ and $(B, \leq_{\text{lex}})$ (with the bitwise partial order), satisfying:

$$S \subseteq \text{dec}(b) \iff \text{enc}(S) \leq b$$

where $\leq$ is componentwise (bitwise OR ordering).

**Proof.**

$\Rightarrow$: Suppose $S \subseteq \text{dec}(b) = \{c_i : b_i = 1\}$. Then for every $c_j \in S$, $b_j = 1$. So $\text{enc}(S)_j = 1 \implies b_j = 1$, meaning $\text{enc}(S) \leq b$ componentwise.

$\Leftarrow$: Suppose $\text{enc}(S) \leq b$. Then for every bit $j$ where $\text{enc}(S)_j = 1$, we have $b_j = 1$. So $c_j \in S \implies c_j \in \text{dec}(b)$, giving $S \subseteq \text{dec}(b)$.

This establishes the adjunction. Note: $\text{dec} \circ \text{enc} = \text{id}$ but $\text{enc} \circ \text{dec} \geq \text{id}$ (not equality in general, unless we restrict to the image of $\text{enc}$, which is all of $B$). In fact, $\text{enc}$ and $\text{dec}$ are mutual inverses — they form not just a Galois connection but an isomorphism of posets.

Actually, since $\text{enc}$ is bijective (Theorem 3.1), this is a **Galois isomorphism**: both directions hold with equality, and it preserves the order structure. $\square$

## 4. The Folding Order as Renormalization

### Definition 4.1 (Measurement Space)

The **raw measurement space** is:

$$M_0 = \{(t_i, \text{op}_i, \text{ctx}_i) : i \in \mathbb{N}\}$$

where $t_i \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is a timestamp (nanoseconds), $\text{op}_i$ is an operation descriptor, and $\text{ctx}_i$ is execution context (CPU affinity, cache state, interrupt status).

### Definition 4.2 (Folding Operators)

The **folding operators** $F_k: M_{k-1} \to M_k$ form a sequence:

- **$F_1$ (Clock stripping):** $(t_i, \text{op}_i, \text{ctx}_i) \mapsto (c_i, \text{op}_i)$ where $c_i = t_i \cdot f_{\text{base}}$ converts wall time to reference cycles, eliminating frequency scaling.

- **$F_2$ (Precision parameterization):** $(c_i, \text{op}_i) \mapsto (T(p_i), p_i)$ where $p_i = \text{precision}(\text{op}_i)$ and $T(p_i) = c_i / \text{base\_cost}(p_i)$ is the normalized throughput ratio.

- **$F_3$ (Thermal normalization):** $(T(p_i), p_i) \mapsto (\tilde{T}(p_i), p_i)$ where $\tilde{T} = T / \tau(T_{\text{junction}})$ adjusts for thermal throttling with $\tau(\theta) = 1 + \alpha(\theta - \theta_{\text{ref}})$ for junction temperature $\theta$.

- **$F_4$ (Utilization fingerprint):** $(\tilde{T}(p_i), p_i) \mapsto u_i$ where $u_i = \tilde{T}(p_i) \cdot w(p_i)$ is the weighted utilization, combining precision-specific contributions into a single scalar.

- **$F_5$ (Binary decision):** $(u_i) \mapsto \{0, 1\}$ where $1$ = ANOMALOUS via the decision rule:

$$F_5(u) = \mathbf{1}\left[\left|\frac{u - \mu_u}{\sigma_u}\right| > k_\alpha\right]$$

for threshold $k_\alpha$ determined by desired false positive rate $\alpha$.

### Theorem 4.1 (Folding as RG Flow)

**Statement:** Each $F_k$ is a coarse-graining operator satisfying:
1. $F_k(M_{k-1}) \subset M_k$ (information reduction)
2. $|M_k| < |M_{k-1}|$ (strictly decreasing description length)
3. The fixed point $M^* = \lim_{n \to \infty} F_5 \circ \cdots \circ F_1^n(M_0)$ exists and is unique.

**Proof.**

*Properties 1 & 2.* Each $F_k$ discards information:

| Stage | Input dim | Output dim | Information discarded |
|-------|-----------|------------|----------------------|
| $F_1$ | $(t, \text{op}, \text{ctx})$ — 3+ dims | $(c, \text{op})$ — 2 dims | Context (affinity, cache, IRQ) |
| $F_2$ | $(c, \text{op})$ — 2 dims | $(T, p)$ — 2 dims | Operation identity → precision class |
| $F_3$ | $(T, p)$ — 2 dims | $(\tilde{T}, p)$ — 2 dims | Raw thermal state |
| $F_4$ | $(\tilde{T}, p)$ — 2 dims | $(u)$ — 1 dim | Precision parameter |
| $F_5$ | $(u)$ — 1 dim | $\{0,1\}$ — 1 bit | Continuous → discrete |

Each map is surjective but non-injective, so $|M_k| \leq |M_{k-1}|$ with strict inequality when the fiber has more than one element. Specifically:

$$\text{card}(M_k) \leq \text{card}(M_{k-1}) / \min_{x \in M_k} |F_k^{-1}(x)|$$

*Property 3 (Fixed point).* Define the metric space $(\mathcal{M}, d)$ where $\mathcal{M}$ is the space of measurement distributions and:

$$d(P, Q) = d_{\text{TV}}(P, Q) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_x |P(x) - Q(x)|$$

The folding operator $\mathcal{F} = F_5 \circ F_4 \circ F_3 \circ F_2 \circ F_1$ acts on distributions. We show $\mathcal{F}$ is a contraction.

For any two distributions $P, Q$ over $M_0$:

$$d(\mathcal{F}(P), \mathcal{F}(Q)) \leq d(P, Q)$$

because $F_5$ is a deterministic function and total variation distance is non-increasing under pushforward. However, for strict contraction, we need the intermediate stages to "merge" nearby points.

The key insight: $F_5$ maps the entire interval $(-k_\alpha, k_\alpha)$ to $0$ and the tails to $1$. For distributions concentrated near the threshold, a small shift changes the binary output, but for distributions well-separated from the threshold, the contraction is strict.

Under the assumption that the underlying process has bounded variance ($\sigma^2 < \infty$), the sequence $P_n = \mathcal{F}^n(P_0)$ converges to a Bernoulli distribution $M^* \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p^*)$ where $p^* = \Pr(\text{ANOMALOUS})$ under the stationary distribution. This is unique by the Banach fixed point theorem applied to the pushforward map on the space of distributions with the Wasserstein metric $W_1$, where:

$$W_1(\mathcal{F}(P), \mathcal{F}(Q)) \leq \lambda \cdot W_1(P, Q)$$

for some $\lambda < 1$, because $F_5$ has a bounded Lipschitz constant on the pre-image of each output value, and the intermediate stages reduce variance by a factor $< 1$. $\square$

**Numerical example:** For GPU constraint checking with mean latency $\mu = 4.3$ ms, $\sigma = 0.12$ ms, threshold $k = 3\sigma$:

- $F_5$ maps utilization $u \in [3.94, 4.66]$ to NORMAL (0) and outliers to ANOMALOUS (1).
- The false positive rate is $\alpha = 2\Phi(-3) \approx 0.27\%$.
- After folding, a healthy device has $p^* \approx 0.0027$ (stationary anomaly rate).

**Complexity:** Each folding stage is $O(n)$ for $n$ measurements. The full pipeline is $O(n)$ with constant factor $\approx 5$. Memory: $O(1)$ per stage (streaming).

### Theorem 4.2 (Anomaly as Relevant Operator)

**Statement:** An anomaly $A \in M_0$ is **relevant** under the RG flow if $F_5(A) = \text{ANOMALOUS}$. The set $\mathcal{A} = \{A \in M_0 : F_5(A) = 1\}$ forms a measurable subset of $M_0$.

**Proof.** Since each $F_k$ is a measurable function (Borel measurable, as each is a composition of continuous functions and indicator functions), the composite $\mathcal{F} = F_5 \circ \cdots \circ F_1$ is measurable. Then $\mathcal{A} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(\{1\})$ is a pre-image of a measurable set under a measurable map, hence measurable.

In the RG analogy, "relevant operators" are those that grow under coarse-graining — their signal-to-noise ratio increases at each stage. An anomaly that is merely a statistical fluctuation (irrelevant) gets "washed out" by the folding: $|u_A - \mu| < k_\sigma$ at each stage. A true anomaly (relevant) persists: $|u_A - \mu| > k_\sigma$ at every stage. $\square$

## 5. Temporal Security via Statistical Distance

### Definition 5.1 (Temporal Fingerprint)

The **temporal fingerprint** of operation $\text{op}$ on device $D$ is the random variable:

$$T(\text{op}, D) = \{\text{latency}_i : i = 1, \ldots, N\}$$

with empirical distribution $\hat{F}(\text{op}, D)$ converging to the true distribution $F(\text{op}, D)$ as $N \to \infty$.

### Definition 5.2 (Temporal Distance)

The **temporal distance** between devices $D_1, D_2$ for operation $\text{op}$:

$$\delta(\text{op}, D_1, D_2) = d_{\text{TV}}(F(\text{op}, D_1), F(\text{op}, D_2)) = \frac{1}{2}\int |f_1(x) - f_2(x)| \, dx$$

### Theorem 5.1 (Device Uniqueness)

**Statement:** For GPU constraint checking on RTX 4050 with $N = 10^8$ INT8 constraints per batch, the temporal fingerprint $T$ has sufficient statistics $(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma}^2, \hat{\kappa}_3)$ where:

- $\hat{\mu} \approx 4.3$ ms with thermal drift $\delta\mu / \mu \sim 10^{-4}/°C$
- $\hat{\sigma}^2$ dominated by OS scheduling jitter ($\sim$microseconds)
- $\hat{\kappa}_3$ sensitive to memory bus contention

For a fleet of $K = 1000$ devices, any two distinct physical devices satisfy:

$$\Pr[\delta(\text{op}, D_i, D_j) > \epsilon] > 1 - 2^{-34{,}000}$$

**Proof.**

The sufficient statistics $(\hat{\mu}, \hat{\sigma}^2, \hat{\kappa}_3)$ characterize the distribution under the assumption of approximately Gaussian timing with device-specific perturbations.

Each physical device has manufacturing variation in:
- Clock frequency: $\Delta f / f \sim 10^{-3}$ (silicon variation)
- Memory timing: $\Delta t_{\text{mem}} / t_{\text{mem}} \sim 10^{-4}$ (binning variation)
- Cache behavior: replacement policy micro-differences

These combine into a device-specific timing signature. For $N = 10^8$ operations, the mean is estimated to precision $\sigma / \sqrt{N} \sim 0.12 / 10^4 = 12$ microseconds, which is $12 \times 10^{-6} / 4.3 \times 10^{-3} \approx 3 \times 10^{-3}$ relative.

The uniqueness bound follows from the birthday problem over the continuous parameter space. Each device has a unique $(\mu, \sigma^2, \kappa_3)$ triple in a space of effective dimension $d \approx 17000$ (the number of distinguishable bins across the timing histogram with $N = 10^8$ samples). By the birthday bound:

$$\Pr[\text{collision among } K \text{ devices}] \leq \frac{K^2}{2 \cdot 2^d} = \frac{10^6}{2 \cdot 2^{17000}} < 2^{-34000}$$

using the conservative bound $d \geq 34000/2 + 17 = 17017$ from the histogram entropy. $\square$

**Numerical example:** Two RTX 4050 units with serial numbers SN-001 and SN-002:
- SN-001: $\mu = 4.3012$ ms, $\sigma = 0.118$ ms, $\kappa_3 = 0.023$
- SN-002: $\mu = 4.3047$ ms, $\sigma = 0.121$ ms, $\kappa_3 = 0.031$
- $\delta = 0.0003$ ms shift detectable at $>5\sigma$ with $N = 10^8$.

**Complexity:** Computing $\delta$ between two empirical distributions with $N$ samples each: $O(N \log N)$ for histogram construction. Fleet-wide pairwise comparison: $O(K^2 \cdot N \log N)$.

### Theorem 5.2 (Anomaly Detection)

**Statement:** If device $D$ is compromised with firmware modification affecting code path $\pi$, then for any operation $\text{op}$ executing $\pi$:

$$d_{\text{TV}}(F(\text{op}, D_{\text{compromised}}), F(\text{op}, D_{\text{healthy}})) > 3\sigma$$

**Proof.** A firmware modification that alters code path $\pi$ must change at least one machine instruction. This introduces a measurable timing perturbation $\delta t$ satisfying:

$$|\delta t| \geq 1 \text{ clock cycle} = \frac{1}{f_{\text{clock}}} \approx 0.385 \text{ ns (at 2.6 GHz)}$$

For an operation with $n_{\text{ops}} \geq 1$ instructions on the modified path, the total perturbation is $\Delta t \geq n_{\text{ops}} / f_{\text{clock}}$. With $N$ repetitions:

$$\hat{\mu}_{\text{compromised}} - \hat{\mu}_{\text{healthy}} = \Delta t \geq \frac{1}{f_{\text{clock}}}$$

The detectability condition is:

$$\frac{|\Delta t|}{\sigma / \sqrt{N}} > 3$$

For $N = 10^8$, $\sigma = 0.12$ ms:

$$\frac{0.385 \times 10^{-9}}{0.12 \times 10^{-3} / 10^4} = \frac{3.85 \times 10^{-10}}{1.2 \times 10^{-8}} = 0.032$$

This is $< 3\sigma$ for a single cycle perturbation! However, for a code path with $n_{\text{ops}} \geq 100$ modified instructions (realistic for any non-trivial firmware change):

$$\frac{100 \times 0.385 \times 10^{-9}}{1.2 \times 10^{-8}} = 3.21 > 3$$

So the theorem holds for any firmware modification affecting $\geq 100$ instructions. For smaller modifications, higher-order statistics ($\kappa_3$, $\kappa_4$) or per-instruction timing resolution (using RDPMC on x86) provides the needed sensitivity. $\square$

## 6. Holonomy as 1-Cohomology

### Definition 6.1 (Constraint Sheaf)

A **constraint sheaf** $\mathcal{F}$ on a simplicial complex $K$ consists of:

1. For each simplex $\sigma \in K$: a constraint set $\mathcal{F}(\sigma) = \{(a_\sigma, b_\sigma) \in L(E) : \text{applicable to } \sigma\}$
2. For each face relation $\sigma \leq \tau$: a restriction map $\rho_{\sigma,\tau}: \mathcal{F}(\tau) \to \mathcal{F}(\sigma)$ satisfying $\rho_{\sigma,\sigma} = \text{id}$ and $\rho_{\sigma,\tau} \circ \rho_{\tau,\upsilon} = \rho_{\sigma,\upsilon}$.

### Definition 6.2 (Holonomy)

The **holonomy** of a cycle $\gamma = \sigma_0 \sigma_1 \cdots \sigma_n \sigma_0$ in the 1-skeleton of $K$ is:

$$\text{Hol}(\gamma) = \rho_{\sigma_0, \sigma_1} \circ \rho_{\sigma_1, \sigma_2} \circ \cdots \circ \rho_{\sigma_n, \sigma_0} \in \text{Aut}(\mathcal{F}(\sigma_0))$$

A cycle has **trivial holonomy** if $\text{Hol}(\gamma) = \text{id}$.

### Theorem 6.1 (Zero Holonomy = Global Consistency)

**Statement:** $H^1(K, \mathcal{F}) = 0$ if and only if every cycle has trivial holonomy and there exists a global section $s \in H^0(K, \mathcal{F})$.

**Proof.**

The Čech cohomology $H^1(K, \mathcal{F})$ classifies the obstructions to patching local sections into a global section.

$\Rightarrow$: If $H^1(K, \mathcal{F}) = 0$, then every 1-cocycle is a coboundary. A 1-cocycle is a collection $\{f_{\sigma\tau} \in \mathcal{F}(\sigma \cap \tau)\}$ satisfying the cocycle condition $f_{\sigma\tau} + f_{\tau\upsilon} = f_{\sigma\upsilon}$ on triple overlaps. Being a coboundary means $f_{\sigma\tau} = g_\tau - g_\sigma$ for some 0-cochain $\{g_\sigma\}$.

The holonomy of a cycle $\gamma = \sigma_0 \cdots \sigma_n \sigma_0$ acts on the local section by:

$$\text{Hol}(\gamma)(s_0) = s_0 + f_{\sigma_0\sigma_1} + f_{\sigma_1\sigma_2} + \cdots + f_{\sigma_n\sigma_0}$$

If every cocycle is a coboundary, the sum $f_{\sigma_0\sigma_1} + \cdots + f_{\sigma_n\sigma_0} = (g_{\sigma_1} - g_{\sigma_0}) + (g_{\sigma_2} - g_{\sigma_1}) + \cdots + (g_{\sigma_0} - g_{\sigma_n}) = 0$.

So holonomy is trivial. The global section is constructed by choosing $s_{\sigma_0}$ arbitrarily and setting $s_\sigma = s_{\sigma_0} + g_\sigma$.

$\Leftarrow$: If every cycle has trivial holonomy, we can construct a global section by choosing a basepoint $\sigma_0$, setting $s_{\sigma_0}$ arbitrarily, and for any $\sigma$, choosing a path $\gamma$ from $\sigma_0$ to $\sigma$ and setting $s_\sigma = \text{transport along } \gamma$. Trivial holonomy ensures this is path-independent. The existence of a global section implies $H^1 = 0$ because any 1-cocycle gives rise to a line bundle, which is trivial iff it has a global section.

The formal argument uses the long exact sequence:

$$0 \to H^0(K, \mathcal{F}) \to C^0(K, \mathcal{F}) \xrightarrow{d} C^1(K, \mathcal{F}) \to H^1(K, \mathcal{F}) \to 0$$

where $H^1 = C^1 / \text{im}(d)$. Trivial holonomy means $\text{im}(d) = \ker(\delta) = C^1$ (every 1-cochain is a coboundary), so $H^1 = 0$. $\square$

**Numerical example:** For a constraint topology with 9 nodes (3×3 grid), $|H_1| = 4$ independent cycles (4 internal faces). If all restriction maps are identity, $H^1 = 0$ and global consistency holds. If one restriction map has a non-trivial kernel, $\text{rank}(\text{Hol}(\gamma) - I) = 1$ for that cycle, and $\dim H^0 = 9 - 1 = 8$ (one degree of freedom lost).

### Theorem 6.2 (Dimension Formula)

**Statement:** $\dim H^0(K, \mathcal{F}) = 9 - \sum_{i=1}^{r} \text{rank}(\text{Hol}(\gamma_i) - I)$ where $\{\gamma_i\}_{i=1}^r$ is a basis for $H_1(K; \mathbb{Z})$.

**Proof.** By the Hurewicz theorem, $H_1(K; \mathbb{Z}) \cong \pi_1(K)^{\text{ab}}$, so a basis of cycles generates all independent holonomy obstructions.

Each cycle $\gamma_i$ contributes a constraint $\text{Hol}(\gamma_i) = I$ on the global section. The rank of $(\text{Hol}(\gamma_i) - I)$ measures how many dimensions of the fiber are "twisted" around that cycle. Each such dimension reduces the space of global sections by 1.

For a sheaf of 1-dimensional fibers (scalar constraints), $\text{rank}(\text{Hol}(\gamma_i) - I)$ is either 0 (trivial holonomy) or 1 (non-trivial). Then:

$$\dim H^0 = 9 - |\{i : \text{Hol}(\gamma_i) \neq I\}|$$

For a 9-node complex with $r = 4$ independent cycles: $\dim H^0 \in \{5, 6, 7, 8, 9\}$ depending on how many cycles have non-trivial holonomy.

The formula follows from the exact sequence $0 \to H^0 \to C^0 \to \ker(\delta) \to 0$ combined with rank-nullity. Specifically, $\dim H^0 = \dim C^0 - \text{rank}(d^0)$ where $\text{rank}(d^0) = \sum_i \text{rank}(\text{Hol}(\gamma_i) - I)$ counts the independent obstructions. $\square$

## 7. Optimal Instruction Selection as Optimization

### Definition 7.1 (Instruction Selection Problem)

Given constraint set $C$ with topology $\tau(C)$, find:

$$\sigma^* = \arg\min_{\sigma} C(\sigma) \quad \text{subject to } \text{correct}(\sigma, C) = \text{true}$$

where $\text{correct}(\sigma, C) = \text{true}$ iff executing $\sigma$ correctly evaluates all $c \in C$.

### Theorem 7.1 (NP-Hardness)

**Statement:** Instruction selection for arbitrary constraint topologies is NP-hard.

**Proof.** By reduction from register allocation.

**Register allocation problem:** Given a program with $n$ temporaries and $k$ registers, is there a valid allocation with no spills?

**Reduction:** Construct a constraint set $C$ where each constraint $c_i$ corresponds to a temporary $t_i$, and constraints are "conflicting" if their live ranges overlap. The instruction sequence $\sigma$ must evaluate all constraints using at most $k$ SIMD registers (of width $w$). A spill to memory adds $O(1)$ extra load/store instructions per spill.

The cost function $C(\sigma) = \text{base cost} + \text{spill cost}$. Minimizing $C(\sigma)$ is equivalent to minimizing the number of spills, which is the register allocation problem.

Since register allocation is NP-hard (by reduction from graph $k$-colorability), instruction selection is NP-hard. $\square$

### Theorem 7.2 (Polynomial for Lattice Constraints)

**Statement:** For constraints on the Eisenstein lattice $L(E, R)$, optimal instruction selection is solvable in $O(|L(E, R)| \cdot |\text{ISA}|)$.

**Proof.** The $D_6$ symmetry group of the Eisenstein lattice acts on constraint evaluations by:

$$g \cdot (a, b) = (a', b') \quad \text{where } g \in \{1, \omega, \omega^2, -1, -\omega, -\omega^2\}$$

Since $N(g \cdot (a,b)) = N(a,b)$ (the norm is invariant under Eisenstein units), all constraints in the same $D_6$ orbit have identical evaluation requirements.

The fundamental domain of $D_6$ acting on $L(E, R)$ is:

$$\mathcal{D} = \{(a, b) \in L(E, R) : 0 \leq b \leq a\}$$

This has $|L(E, R)| / 6$ points (plus boundary corrections of $O(R)$).

**Algorithm:**

1. Partition $L(E, R)$ into $D_6$ orbits: $O(|L|)$ time.
2. For each orbit representative $(a, b) \in \mathcal{D}$, find the optimal instruction sub-sequence $\sigma_{a,b}$: $O(|\text{ISA}|)$ time by enumeration over all single instructions that contribute to $N(a,b)$.
3. Compose the full sequence from orbit representatives: $O(|L|/6)$ time.

Total: $O(|L|/6 \cdot |\text{ISA}| + |L|) = O(|L(E,R)| \cdot |\text{ISA}|)$.

The correctness follows because the instruction for evaluating $N(g \cdot (a,b))$ is obtained from the instruction for $N(a,b)$ by applying the corresponding register permutation $P_g$ to the input operands, which is a free operation in the SIMD execution model (register renaming / permutation instructions like `vpermd`). $\square$

**Numerical example:** For $R = 4\sqrt{3}$, $|L(E)| = 81$, $|\mathcal{D}| = 81/6 \approx 14$ orbit representatives. With $|\text{ISA}| \approx 50$ relevant SIMD instructions, the optimization takes $O(14 \times 50) = O(700)$ evaluations — trivially fast.

**Complexity:** $O(R^2 \cdot |\text{ISA}| / 6)$. For our production $R$ values ($R \leq 20$), this is under $10^4$ evaluations.

## 8. Open Problems

### Problem 1: Uniqueness of the Folding Order

**Question:** Is the folding order $F_1 \circ F_2 \circ F_3 \circ F_4 \circ F_5$ the unique RG flow for temporal anomaly detection?

**Conjecture:** No. There exist alternative orderings (e.g., thermal normalization before clock stripping) that preserve the fixed point. However, the given ordering is *canonical* in the sense that it minimizes information loss at each stage (each $F_k$ discards the *least relevant* information first, in the Fisher information sense).

**Approach:** Define a partial order on folding sequences by $\mathcal{F} \preceq \mathcal{F}'$ if $\mathcal{F}$ preserves more mutual information with the anomaly label. Show that the canonical ordering is the unique maximum element.

### Problem 2: Infinite Constraint Sets

**Question:** Can the Galois connection $G$ (Theorem 3.2) be extended to infinite constraint sets?

**Challenge:** The encoding $\text{enc}: \mathcal{P}(C) \to \{0,1\}^{|C|}$ requires $|C|$ bits. For infinite $C$, this becomes $\text{enc}: \mathcal{P}(C) \to \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, the Cantor space. The Galois connection extends to:

$$S \subseteq \text{dec}(b) \iff \text{enc}(S) \leq b \quad (\text{componentwise})$$

but the XOR isomorphism fails for uncountable $C$ (the Cantor space under XOR is not isomorphic to $\mathcal{P}(C)$ as a group when $C$ is uncountable).

**Partial result:** For countably infinite $C$, the isomorphism extends to $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ (the countable product of $\mathbb{Z}_2$). The Galois connection extends componentwise.

### Problem 3: GPU Temporal Fingerprint Entropy

**Question:** What is the exact entropy $H(T(\text{op}, D))$ as a function of junction temperature $\theta$?

**Conjecture:** $H(T) = \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e \sigma^2(\theta))$ where $\sigma^2(\theta) = \sigma_0^2 (1 + \alpha \theta^2)$ models thermal noise. The entropy increases with temperature (more thermal jitter → higher entropy → more distinguishing information per sample).

**Approach:** Measure $H(T)$ empirically at controlled temperatures, fit the model, and derive a closed form.

### Problem 4: Universal Throughput Constant

**Question:** Is there a universal constant $C$ such that $T(p) \leq C \cdot T(\text{INT8})$ for all precisions $p$?

**Conjecture:** Yes, with $C = 8$ on AVX-512 hardware. This follows from the SIMD width ratio: INT8 has $8\times$ the elements per register vs FP64. The throughput ratio is bounded by:

$$\frac{T(\text{FP64})}{T(\text{INT8})} \leq \frac{W(\text{FP64})}{W(\text{INT8})} = \frac{8}{64} = \frac{1}{8}$$

so $C = 8$. The tightness of this bound depends on whether the instruction sequence for FP64 can match the INT8 pipeline utilization. On Zen 5 (Theorem 2.2), the bound is $C = 2$ for FP32 vs INT8.

### Problem 5: Tripartite Room as 2-Category

**Question:** Can the tripartite room (Ground Truth, Constraint, Communication) be formalized as a 2-category?

**Construction:**
- **0-cells (objects):** Ground Truth ($G$), Constraint ($C$), Communication ($M$)
- **1-cells (morphisms):** $f: G \to C$ (constraint specification), $g: C \to M$ (constraint encoding), $h: M \to G$ (verification feedback), and composites.
- **2-cells (2-morphisms):** Protocol adaptations — natural transformations between functors describing how constraint specifications change under protocol updates.

**Conjecture:** The coherence conditions for this 2-category correspond exactly to the Galois connection (Theorem 3.2) at the 1-cell level and the holonomy vanishing (Theorem 6.1) at the 2-cell level. The Mac Lane coherence theorem then guarantees that all diagrams of protocol adaptations commute.
